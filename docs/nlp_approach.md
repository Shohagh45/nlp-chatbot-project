# NLP Approach: Design Choices and Trade-offs

This project is a retrieval-augmented chatbot that answers questions about EU artificial intelligence policy, grounding every reply in primary sources (the AI Act, EDPS and EDPB guidance, JRC reports, and European Parliament briefings) rather than in the language model's own memory. The bot does not generate opinions or invent facts; it retrieves relevant passages from real documents and uses them to inform its replies. This document goes beyond the README's overview. Where the README states each design decision in a paragraph, here we work through the underlying mathematics, the trade-offs we weighed, and the alternatives we considered and ultimately rejected. See the README's "NLP Approach" section for a one-paragraph version of each topic below.

## 1. Text Embeddings: Why nomic-embed-text and 768 Dimensions

An embedding is a function `f(text) -> R^d` that maps a piece of text to a point in a `d`-dimensional real vector space, arranged so that semantically similar text lands at nearby points. The useful property is that geometric distance in this space approximates semantic distance in meaning. Two passages that say the same thing in different words sit close together even when they share no vocabulary, and two passages that share vocabulary but mean different things sit far apart. Retrieval then becomes a geometry problem: embed the question, and find the document chunks whose points are closest to it.

We picked `nomic-embed-text` for three reasons. It is open-weight, so there is no API key and no per-call cost. It was trained on a broad English corpus that includes formal, legal, and regulatory text, which is a close match for the register of EU policy documents (dense, clause-heavy, and full of defined terms). And it runs locally through Ollama, which keeps the whole system reproducible on a grader's machine with no network dependency.

The 768-dimension choice is a capacity versus compute trade-off. More dimensions give the model room to encode finer semantic distinctions, but they also enlarge every stored vector and slow every search. At our scale the arithmetic is reassuring. With roughly 2000 chunks, the embedding matrix is about 2000 by 768 single-precision floats, which is close to 6 MB. That fits comfortably in RAM, and a search across the whole matrix is effectively instant. The dimension count is high enough to separate policy themes cleanly and low enough that nothing about storage or speed becomes a concern.

L2 normalisation is where a small piece of mathematics pays off. For two vectors `a` and `b`, cosine similarity is defined as `cosine(a, b) = (a . b) / (||a|| * ||b||)`. If both vectors already have unit length, the two norms in the denominator are each 1, so the expression collapses to `cosine(a, b) = a . b`, the plain dot product. We normalise every chunk vector once, at embed time, so the division never has to happen again during search. This is a precomputation, not an approximation: the ranking it produces is identical to computing full cosine similarity, just with half the arithmetic per query and no per-element division in the hot path.

We considered several alternatives. OpenAI's `text-embedding-3-small` and the older `ada-002` are well-tuned and would likely improve raw retrieval quality, but each requires an API key and a network call, which breaks reproducibility on a grader's machine and conflicts with the assignment's request for self-contained local models. Multilingual embeddings such as `paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM` solve a problem we do not have, since our indexed corpus is English-only, and they spend capacity on languages we never query. Sentence-BERT models such as `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` are smaller and faster at 384 dimensions, but in our smoke tests the larger nomic model produced noticeably better separation between policy themes, which matters more than a few milliseconds of search time at this corpus size.

## 2. Chunking Strategy: 500 Words, 50 Overlap, Paragraph-Preferred

Chunk size is a precision versus recall tension in disguise. Small chunks have tight topical focus, so when one is retrieved it is almost entirely on-topic, which helps retrieval precision. But a small chunk strips away the surrounding context that the language model needs to write a coherent answer, so the reply can end up technically grounded yet thin. Large chunks have the opposite failure mode: they carry plenty of context but mix several topics, so a retrieved chunk is only partly relevant and the on-topic sentences get diluted.

We target about 500 words per chunk as the midpoint of that tension. The token budget confirms the choice is safe. English policy text runs at roughly 1.3 tokens per word, so 500 words is approximately 650 tokens. That sits well inside the 8192-token input limit of `nomic-embed-text`, and it is small enough that the retrieved top-six chunks together leave ample room inside `granite4.1`'s 128K-token context window for several turns of conversation history.

The 50-word overlap addresses a specific defect. A sentence or argument that straddles a chunk boundary would otherwise be cut in half, and neither half ranks well as a retrieval result because neither contains the complete thought. By overlapping consecutive chunks by 50 words, any information sitting near a boundary appears in both adjacent chunks, so no sentence is permanently lost to an unlucky split.

Boundary preference follows a simple ranking: paragraph splits are better than sentence splits, which are better than mid-word cuts. A paragraph break preserves the structure of an argument; a sentence break at least preserves one complete thought; a mid-word cut destroys both and produces a fragment that embeds poorly. The splitter always prefers the strongest available boundary within a window of about 50 words around the target chunk size, rather than slicing rigidly at the 500-word mark.

A 100-character minimum filter removes the debris. Chunks shorter than this are usually headers, footnotes, page numbers, or extraction noise. They retrieve unpredictably and waste a slot in the top-k that a real passage could have used.

Among alternatives, sentence-level chunking (one sentence per chunk) is too granular: retrieved excerpts lose their context and the chunk count balloons, which slows everything downstream. Document-level chunking (one chunk per document) is too coarse: retrieval degenerates into a glorified document search, and the language model has to wade through long stretches of irrelevant text to find the relevant clause. Semantic chunking, which splits on topic boundaries detected from embedding similarity, is more sophisticated and genuinely attractive, but it adds implementation complexity and a tunable threshold, and at our corpus size the gain over a well-tuned fixed-size splitter is marginal.

## 3. Vector Search: Brute-Force Dot Product over a Pre-Normalised Matrix

The storage layout is deliberately flat. All chunk embeddings live in a single `(N, 768)` float32 matrix saved to `embeddings.npz`, and `chunks.json` holds the metadata for each chunk, row-aligned so that row `i` of the matrix always corresponds to entry `i` of the JSON. Keeping the two in lockstep means a retrieved row index immediately yields its source document, title, theme, and text with no lookup table.

The search itself is one line of intent. We compute `scores = embeddings @ query_vec`, a single matrix-vector product that returns the cosine similarity (because both sides are unit-normalised) between the query and every chunk at once. This is `O(N * D)` floating-point operations with no Python-level loop, dispatched straight to the BLAS routines NumPy sits on top of, so it runs at hardware speed.

Selecting the top results uses `np.argpartition(scores, -k)` rather than `np.argsort(scores)`. A full sort is `O(N log N)`, but we do not need the whole array ordered, only the `k` best entries. Argpartition runs in `O(N)` to place the top `k` (unordered) at the end of the array, after which we sort just those `k`, giving `O(N + k log k)` overall. At `N = 2000` the constant factors dominate and either approach feels instant, but choosing argpartition signals that we understand where the asymptotic cost actually lives.

The 0.25 similarity threshold filters out weak matches before they reach the language model. The number is not magic. It is the empirical floor above which our smoke queries returned chunks that were on-topic most of the time. We treat it as tunable and say so in the code and tests: a larger or noisier corpus would shift the distribution of scores and merit a fresh look at this cutoff.

We deliberately did not reach for an approximate nearest-neighbour index such as HNSW, IVF, or FAISS. Those structures earn their keep when an exhaustive scan exceeds the query latency budget, which typically happens somewhere above a million vectors. At 2000 chunks our exhaustive search completes in single-digit milliseconds, so an approximate index would buy us nothing while introducing a recall-versus-latency trade-off and an extra dependency. We also did not use SQLite or a hosted vector database such as Pinecone, Weaviate, or pgvector for the vectors, because the assignment explicitly requires the vector layer to be built from scratch in pure Python with NumPy. SQLite does appear in the project, in `src/database.py`, but only for document metadata, never for the embeddings.

## 4. RAG Architecture: Retrieve-then-Generate, Top-6, Temperature 0.3

The pipeline follows the retrieve-then-generate paradigm. At query time we embed the user's message, retrieve the top-k chunks by similarity, format them into a context block, and pass both that block and the original question to the language model. The crucial consequence is epistemic: the model never produces grounded claims from anything except its base training and the chunks retrieved on the current turn, which is what lets us insist on citations and honesty about gaps.

We retrieve six chunks rather than three or ten because six is an empirical midpoint. Too few, and the model occasionally runs out of relevant material on a multi-faceted question and starts to improvise. Too many, and the context block becomes a wall of text in which the single most relevant chunk gets diluted among weaker neighbours. Six provides enough coverage for layered questions while leaving room in the 128K context window for several turns of history.

The context block is numbered and annotated, with each entry formatted as `[1] Title - Publisher (similarity=0.43)` followed by the chunk text. The model therefore sees the source attribution and a confidence signal right next to the excerpt, and the numbering makes inline citation straightforward to produce.

Temperature is set to 0.3. That is low enough to keep the persona stable from turn to turn, with no wild stylistic swings, and high enough that phrasing varies naturally instead of reading like a template. A temperature of 0.0 would make the bot sound like a search result, and 0.7 or higher would let the carefully designed persona drift. We do not stream tokens, because the CLI does not need it and a single non-streaming reply against `granite4.1:3b` on CPU takes roughly 2 to 4 seconds, which is fine for an academic demo. Streaming would add complexity to both the CLI and the Streamlit layer for no real gain in this setting.

## 5. Prompt Engineering: The Validation-First Persona

The system prompt is organised into four parts. WHO YOU ARE introduces the assistant and builds in epistemic humility: it can be wrong, it has no memory between sessions, and it only relays what the documents say. YOUR MANNER asks it to validate before it reasons, keep replies short, and close with a follow-up question. USING THE RETRIEVED DOCUMENTS instructs it to treat excerpts as background rather than a quoted wall of text, cite by title and publisher, and never fabricate. STAYING IN SCOPE tells it to decline off-topic requests warmly.

Validating emotion before turning to policy detail is a deliberate design choice rather than a courtesy. People who ask about AI harm, surveillance, or automated decisions are often anxious, and a clinical "Here are the regulations" reply lands as cold and confirms the suspicion that the system is just another impersonal machine. Acknowledging the person's situation in a single sentence first makes the substantive part of the reply land far better. The conversation examples in the assignment's scenario files were the direct design source for this pattern.

Ending with a follow-up question keeps the conversation open and avoids the info-dump-and-stop pattern that turns an assistant into a stiff question-and-answer box. The model is explicitly told to ask a real, short question that moves things forward, not a hollow "Is there anything else I can help with?". Citations are by document title and publisher rather than internal chunk identifiers, because users care which document a claim came from, not which internal slice of it. The chunk-level dedupe in `app.py` and the Streamlit Sources expander both group by `doc_id`, so the citation list a user sees is per-document.

Off-topic refusal is a soft boundary on purpose. The instruction is to decline warmly and briefly, explain that the request is outside scope, and steer back, all without breaking character. A hard refusal would make the bot brittle whenever a user makes a tangential reference, whereas a soft refusal preserves the thread of the conversation.

## 6. Conversation Memory: Rolling Window of 8 Messages

History is kept as a rolling window of the last 8 messages, which is 4 exchanges. Granite4.1's 128K-token context window is large enough that we can afford this directly, without any summarisation machinery.

We chose not to summarise older history. Summarisation would add a second language-model call on every turn, it is itself a source of error and drift, and it simply is not necessary at the conversation lengths an academic demo produces. Systems that hold conversations for hours genuinely need it; this one does not.

What we store is the raw user message and the assistant reply, not the retrieval-augmented prompt. Storing the augmented prompt would balloon the history with repeated excerpts, and the model gains nothing from seeing prior retrievals when answering a follow-up, because every turn performs its own fresh retrieval. This is also why follow-ups resolve naturally. When a user asks "and what about children?" after a question about deepfakes, the earlier user message and reply are still in the window, so the model can bind "children" to "deepfakes" with no special handling from us. The fresh retrieval for the follow-up query also tends to surface chunks about deepfakes involving children, because the conversational context shapes how the follow-up embeds.

## 7. Why "From Scratch" Matters

The academic argument for hand-building the vector layer in NumPy is that it makes the abstractions concrete. You can point at one line in `vector_db.py` and say truthfully "this is similarity search," and at another and say "this is top-k selection." With a framework like LangChain or a library like FAISS, those operations disappear behind an interface and the understanding they are meant to demonstrate disappears with them.

In production the calculus flips. We would reach for LangChain or LlamaIndex to handle the orchestration glue, FAISS or pgvector for a real index, and a managed embedding provider such as Cohere or OpenAI for higher quality. None of those are appropriate for a course assignment whose entire purpose is to demonstrate understanding of the primitives. What the from-scratch build gave us is a clean mental model of what an embedding actually is, why cosine similarity reduces to a dot product, where the bottleneck would appear at scale, and how a retrieval-augmented pipeline genuinely differs from raw language-model question answering.

A few things surprised us during implementation. The core search is astonishingly short, essentially one matrix multiply and one partition, which made it clear how much of the heavy lifting NumPy and BLAS quietly absorb. Most of the retrieval quality turned out to live upstream in the chunking and cleaning rather than in the vector layer itself, so the unglamorous preprocessing code mattered more than the search code. And at this corpus size the persona prompt influenced the felt quality of the conversation at least as much as retrieval accuracy did, a reminder that good engineering and good writing are both part of building a usable assistant.
